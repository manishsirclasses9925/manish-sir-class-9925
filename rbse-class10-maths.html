In this chapter, we study prime numbers, prime factorisation, the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic and irrational numbers.

1. Short Notes

Prime Number

A number greater than 1 which has only two factors: 1 and itself.

Examples: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11.

Composite Number

A number greater than 1 which has more than two factors.

Examples: 4, 6, 8, 9, 10.

Co-prime Numbers

Two numbers are co-prime if their HCF is 1.

Example: 8 and 15.

Rational Numbers

A number which can be written in the form p/q, where p and q are integers and q is not zero.

Examples: 1/2, 3/4, 5.

Irrational Numbers

A number which cannot be written in the form p/q.

Examples: √2, √3, √5.

Real Numbers

All rational and irrational numbers together are called real numbers.

2. Important Formulas

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

Every composite number can be expressed as a product of prime numbers, and this factorisation is unique apart from the order of the factors.

HCF × LCM Formula

For two positive integers:

HCF × LCM = Product of the two numbers

Prime Factorisation

Example: 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5

Question 1: Find the prime factorisation of 60.

60 = 2 × 30

Step 2: 30 = 2 × 15

Step 3: 15 = 3 × 5

Question 2: Prove that √2 is irrational.

Step 1: Assume that √2 is rational.
Therefore, √2 = a/b, where a and b are co-prime integers and b ≠ 0.

Step 2: Squaring both sides:
2 = a²/b²
a² = 2b²

Step 3: Therefore, a² is even. So a is also even.
Let a = 2c.

Step 4: Put a = 2c:
4c² = 2b²
b² = 2c²

So b is also even.

Step 5: Both a and b are even. This contradicts our assumption that they are co-prime.

Conclusion: √2 is irrational.
Question 3: Prove that √3 is irrational.

Assume √3 = a/b, where a and b are co-prime integers and b ≠ 0.

Squaring both sides:
3 = a²/b²
a² = 3b²

Therefore, 3 divides a², so 3 also divides a.
Let a = 3c.

Substituting:
9c² = 3b²
b² = 3c²

So 3 also divides b.

This means a and b have a common factor 3, which is a contradiction.

Conclusion: √3 is irrational.
Question 4: Prove that √5 is irrational.

Assume √5 = a/b, where a and b are co-prime integers and b ≠ 0.

Squaring both sides:
5 = a²/b²
a² = 5b²

Therefore, 5 divides a², so 5 divides a.
Let a = 5c.

Substituting:
25c² = 5b²
b² = 5c²

So 5 also divides b.

This contradicts the fact that a and b are co-prime.

Conclusion: √5 is irrational.
